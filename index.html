Senior Design

Eddy Current Probe for Conductivity Measurement

EE 4910 / 4920, Team sdmay27-18
Iowa State University
2026 to 2027

Description

Engineers, researchers, and students often need to measure the electrical conductivity of metal samples, but conductivity equipment is expensive and may not be flexible enough to test different materials and ranges. Our team is designing eddy current probes that work with an affordable vector network analyzer (VNA).

The VNA measures the S-parameters of the probe coils. When a coil is placed near a metal sample, the sample's conductivity and permeability change the coil's complex impedance, and that change is what we measure. The probes are optimized for specific frequency and conductivity ranges and calibrated with reference samples of known conductivity.

  1. Probe design
  2. VNA S-parameters
  3. Impedance change
  4. Range optimization
  5. Calibration
  6. Conductivity result
Problem statement

How might we design an affordable eddy current probe system for engineers measuring the electrical conductivity of metal samples, so that they can obtain accurate and reliable conductivity measurements?

Who
Engineers, researchers, and students who need a reliable, affordable way to measure conductivity
Where
Laboratory and engineering environments where metals are characterized
When
Whenever conductivity must be found, especially across different frequency and conductivity ranges
How
Optimized eddy current probes, VNA S-parameter measurement, and calibration with known references
Team
Benjamin Ponce-Martinez, Truong Luu, Connor Crimmins, Eric Williams, Jonathan Gatewood, and Sebastian De La Torre

Temperature Indicator Circuit

EE 230, Lab 3
With Jesus Robles and Ethan Kriek
Fall 2024

Description

A circuit that reads a temperature sensor signal and lights LEDs to show the temperature range. The green LED is on below 40 °C, the yellow LED turns on above 40 °C and stays on until the temperature falls below 30 °C, and the red LED turns on above 100 °C and stays on until it falls below 80 °C.

Two TL082 op-amps are wired as Schmitt triggers with separate reference voltages. The first switches between 0.3 V and 0.4 V and drives the green and yellow LEDs in an anti-parallel pair. The second switches between 0.8 V and 1.0 V and drives the red LED. The hysteresis keeps each LED from flickering near its threshold.

My role
I calculated the voltage thresholds and helped build and test the circuit. When testing showed the original thresholds were wrong and the output was not what we expected, I suggested changing the resistor values and recalculating the thresholds. We re-tested and got the correct output.
Skills gained
  • Schmitt trigger design and hysteresis
  • Calculating V+, V−, VTH, VTL, resistor ratios, and VREF
  • Comparing design options (we tested two to four op-amp configurations)
  • Finding the gap between calculated and measured switching points
Resources used
TL082 op-amps, resistors, LEDs, variable DC voltage source to stand in for the sensor, oscilloscope, and EE 230 lecture notes.

ii.

Active Audio Crossover Network

EE 230, Lab 6
With Mohamed Eshage
Fall 2024

Description

A circuit that splits one audio signal into three channels for a three-way speaker system: low (below 300 Hz), mid (300 to 5000 Hz), and high (above 5000 Hz).

The low channel is a second-order active low-pass filter with a potentiometer that sets the gain from 1 to 5. The mid channel is a band-pass filter built from a low-pass and a high-pass stage, centered near 1225 Hz. The high channel is a second-order passive high-pass filter with a gain below 1.

My role
I calculated component values and built the low-frequency channel, using three resistors in series to reach the 17 kΩ design value. The first build gave the wrong output voltage and gain, so we tried different parts until a 100 kΩ potentiometer and a 15.68 kΩ resistor gave the correct 1 to 5 adjustable gain. We then measured each channel's frequency response to confirm the design.
Skills gained
  • First- and second-order active and passive filter design
  • Corner frequency, gain, and band-pass center frequency calculations
  • Measuring and plotting frequency response
  • Adjusting a design when measurements don't match the math
Resources used
Op-amps, resistors, capacitors, potentiometers, signal generator, Keysight oscilloscope, Loopinator frequency response software (15 samples from 100 Hz to 10 kHz), and EE 230 lecture slides.

Digital logic

CPRE 288, Team G2

Ethics

The Ethics of Exporting E‑Waste

EE 232 (EE/CPRE 2320)
Final case study

Summary

The United States generates millions of tons of electronic waste each year, and much of it is shipped to developing countries where labor is cheaper and environmental rules are weaker. Workers and nearby communities are exposed to lead, mercury, cadmium, and other toxic substances.

This case study examines the practice through deontological and virtue ethics and the IEEE and NSPE codes of ethics, weighs three possible solutions, and argues that developed countries should regulate or ban e-waste exports, while engineers design longer-lasting, repairable products instead of contributing to planned obsolescence.

Stakeholders
Electronics manufacturers, the federal government, and consumers
Frameworks
Deontological ethics, virtue ethics, IEEE and NSPE codes of ethics
Recommendation
Regulate or ban the export of e-waste through stricter domestic laws
